## Blood loss

I donated blood today. I haven't done that since... 1999? It must have been 1999. I remember traveling east from Lewistown, on US-136, stopping to give blood at an event hosted by one of the Boy Scout troops that camped at Ingersoll, on my way further east to an Order of the Arrow meeting. Or maybe on the way back to U of I. I remember getting really lightheaded after the blood donation, and I've never built up the motivation to try it again.

I got one of those "donate blood" emails at work over the summer and I thought, Sure, I'll do this. I had received those emails before but hadn't jumped in fast enough to secure a spot. This time I got one. Good. Question mark.

Apprehension. I'm not unfamiliar with my own blood. Through clumsiness or inattention, it's had plenty of opportunities to come out and show me. But I remember how bad that felt over a quarter century ago. The cookies and apple juice can only do so much. 

I knew I was in trouble. Get there for the appointment. Fill out the paperwork. The nurse called out that I was doing the Power Red donation and the other nurses called back some sort of cheer. (Maybe they're not capital-N Nurses, but we'll go with it.) Big red flag. No one else got that cheer. What did I sign up for? Did I just pick The Biggest Option to be tough? Possibly. Possibly just clumsiness or inattention. Big red flag number two was the nurse pointing out that I wouldn't be eligible to donate again until March or April or some absurdly long recharge period. Great. Capital-N Nerves.

Sure enough, when the machine paused at the halfway mark of the blood draw, I got to become reacquainted with that old feeling of lightheadedness and a hint of nausea. I don't like it, but it was interesting. It's not the light head of drinking beer. No calming waves of relaxation. No. Nah. This was something strange, something fundamental, something living inside my head and leaning on the wires, shorting some circuits, disconnecting others. 

The nurse lowered the backrest and asked if I wanted a cold compress for my head. Lady, do whatever you think works, I'm barely here. She had a plausible explanation. This happens to people on their first donation. The brain senses that Something Is Wrong, that We Are Losing Blood, and turns to that page of the emergency response plan. I can believe that. It reminds of endurance running. After a few dozen miles, the brain senses that Something Is Wrong, that We Are Losing Energy, and turns to that page of the emergency response plan. There's plenty of energy left in the tank, really, but you have convinced you that there isn't, and you feel bad. Feeling bad should make you stop running, to conserve energy. If you do enough long runs, you can start to tell when the brain is lying or overreacting, and you can press on, pass through the shadows and back into the light.

(It is really irritating me that I can't remember which troop it was, and which city they were from. I know that it was Mike Noble as the Scoutmaster, or at least one of the leaders. II am certain that that troop had one other Scoutmaster that I really liked as well. After years of camp, you get to build these all star rosters of Scoutmasters that you look forward to having back. We must have had that event in Armington, Illinois. That feels right on the map. I don't know if it matters much to lose the details. I'd like to keep them. I haven't thought about camp in ages. )
